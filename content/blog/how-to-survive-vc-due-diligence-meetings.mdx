I spent ninety minutes on a call with the founder of a seed-stage AI startup, and near the end I told them what I had been doing. I had asked them more or less what a VC asks in due diligence.

Some answers came straight away. The one about numbers didn't, and I told them not to worry, because three or four years into my own company I was still asking which numbers we should be looking at.

So this is the list. None of the questions are clever. They are hard only if the first time you hear them is in the meeting.

## What do VCs ask in a due diligence meeting?

They ask about four things, which are the vision, the customers, you and the competition. Every question on this page is a version of one of those, so you can prepare for all of them.

I can't promise every investor sticks to these. But if you can answer the ones on this page out loud without stopping to think, you'll walk in a lot calmer.

## How do you answer "what does this look like if everything goes right?"

Say what the company is doing, who it sells to, and roughly what revenue it makes, in two or three sentences. I ask it as a magic wand question. Everything goes right, so what's the picture?

Then I ask two follow-ups.

- Is there a revenue number on that picture, and by when?
- What is the company doing every day in that picture, and who are its customers?

It's easy to answer the first part with a feeling. An investor wants a company they can see, which means a number, a kind of customer and a thing you do for them.

You don't have to be right. You have to be specific, and you have to sound like you've thought about it before today.

## How do you show an investor you know your customers?

Name them. You should be able to list your top five or ten target customers from memory.

I ask it exactly like that. "Can you list the top five or ten, off the top of your head?" If the list comes out fast, it tells me you've been living in this market. If it comes out slowly, it tells me the target is still an idea.

The follow-up is whether they're all one kind of buyer. Are there neighbouring groups that would buy for the same reason? You want to know the answer, and you want to know which group you're going to first.

There's more on choosing that first group in [how to position a startup](/blog/how-to-position-a-startup).

## How do you talk about your background when you don't have a big track record?

Talk about where you did the work and who you dealt with while you did it. The investor is checking how close you've been to the person who buys.

These are the questions I ask.

- Where did you work when you were doing the hands-on version of this job?
- Who were that firm's clients, and what did the firm do for them?
- Who did you deal with day to day on the client side? Were you close to the work, or was it more hands-off?
- Across your career, which companies and which people did you most enjoy working with?

I got one of these wrong on the call. I assumed the founder had worked at a particular company, and they hadn't. That's fine. They corrected me, and then told me where they had done the work and what their job there was. A plain answer like that is all the question needs.

Then I ask about your network. When you go to a conference or a founders' meetup, who do you end up talking to? Who do you know in the industry?

You don't need famous names for this. An investor wants to know who will take your call.

## How do you answer "who else does this?"

Name the direct competitors, name the big incumbent, and name the customers who build it themselves. I think "nobody does this" is the worst answer you can give.

I ask it in three steps.

- Who else does exactly this today?
- Does the best-known incumbent do it?
- Which firms do you know that are doing it themselves instead of buying?

The third one is easy to skip. Sometimes the real competitor is a spreadsheet, a junior hire or an internal project. If you can say which firms build it themselves and why, the investor can tell you know the market.

## What numbers should you know before the meeting?

Know the one number you most want to move, and how it has changed over the last few weeks. You don't need a dashboard for this. A sheet with a date, a number and one line on why it moved is enough.

I ask it like this. "Do you have a North Star metric? Which number would you most like to see change?"

If you don't have one yet, start now. It takes about an hour to set up, and I've written up how in [data-driven decision making for startups](/blog/data-driven-decision-making-for-startups). The method comes from my [Data-Driven Decision-Making Toolkit](https://gamma.app/docs/Data-Driven-Decision-Making-Toolkit-From-Analytics-to-Visualiza-smz2m6k9t7u666d), which is free to read.

An investor will also ask what would change your mind. The toolkit calls this a decision threshold, which means you write down in advance what result would make you act. It helps to have asked yourself before somebody else does.

## How do you practise for a due diligence meeting?

Get someone to ask you the questions out loud before an investor does. Reading a list in your head is too easy, because you skip the ones you can't answer.

Here is the short version to hand to a friend or an advisor.

1. Everything goes right. What's the picture, and what's the revenue number?
2. List your top five or ten target customers.
3. Where did you do this work before, and who did you deal with?
4. Who do you know in the industry?
5. Who else does this, and who builds it themselves?
6. Which number do you most want to move?

Notice where you slow down. That's the one to work on this week, and [how to prioritize as a startup founder](/blog/how-to-prioritize-as-a-startup-founder) is about choosing it.

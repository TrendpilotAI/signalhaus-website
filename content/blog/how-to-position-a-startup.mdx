I was on a call with the founder of a seed-stage AI startup, and we got onto their website. There were a lot of different things on it. All of them were things the product could do, and it wasn't clear which one a buyer was supposed to pick.

That's a positioning problem, and it's a normal one to have. You built the whole thing, so you want to show the whole thing.

These are the questions I asked to get from the whole thing to one thing.

## How do you work out where to position a startup?

Start where the conversations are already easy. Ask yourself where your message is getting the most traction, and where talking to a buyer doesn't feel like pushing a rock up a hill.

I don't mean easy as in no effort. I mean the calls where the other person gets it quickly and starts telling you about their own version of the problem.

Write down the last few of those. What kind of company were they? What did you say just before they leaned in? That's usually a better guide than the segment you picked on a whiteboard.

## How do you explain what your product does in plain words?

Describe what a customer gets on day one and how they use it. If you can't do that in a few sentences, the buyer can't either.

I play the customer for this. First I say, "I love it. What do I get, and what should we start with?" Then I make it harder. "I've never seen it. I'm taking your word for it and I'm buying. What do I get on day one, and how do I use it?"

The second version is the useful one. It stops you from describing the roadmap, the architecture or the vision. You have to say what turns up and what I do with it.

When the founder told me one part of the product was the strongest, I asked what that part was in plain words. Most of positioning is doing that over and over until a stranger would understand it.

## How do you pick one thing to lead with?

Pick the thing a buyer can say yes or no to straight away. "Do you need this, yes or no?" should have an obvious answer for the right person.

A broad platform makes that hard. The buyer has to work out what it is, where it fits and what it replaces before they can even decide whether they care.

A narrow, well-defined problem is different. They either have it or they don't. If they have it, the next question is how much it costs them today, and now you're in a real conversation.

You're not throwing the rest of the product away. You're choosing the door people come in through. Picking it is a prioritization call as much as a positioning one, and I've written about that in [how to prioritize as a startup founder](/blog/how-to-prioritize-as-a-startup-founder).

## How do you know who your product is for?

Find the point where doing it by hand stops working. Below that point people cope, and above it they start looking for help.

I asked the founder where that line was. Is it a headcount? A volume of work? A number of outside parties the buyer has to deal with? One person with one counterparty can do almost anything manually, or paste it into a chatbot. So where does it start to hurt?

If you can name that line, you know the smallest customer worth talking to. You also know what to ask on a first call to find out which side of the line they're on.

## How should a startup package what it sells?

Sell one engine in the ways people want to buy it. For a lot of AI products that means three ways, which are done for you, self-service, and an API for teams who want to build on it.

This is the idea behind [Services-as-Software](/services). Services can scale like software now, because the work underneath is done by the same system each time.

On the call it came out as three offers from one product.

- A managed service. The customer sends the work and gets the result back.
- A self-service product. The customer logs in and runs it themselves.
- An API. The customer's own developers plug it into what they already have, and pay for what they use.

Each of those is a different buyer with a different reason to say yes. You don't have to launch all three. You do have to know which one you're leading with.

## How do you position against competitors and build-it-yourself?

Know who else does it and who builds it in-house, then give the buyer a way to compare the cost. A simple calculator does this well when the problem is well defined.

The alternatives to buying from you are usually the same three. They can keep doing it by hand, build it themselves, or buy from someone bigger. Each one costs something, in time or in money.

If you can put those next to each other honestly, you don't need to argue. The buyer can see where they are. The competition questions are the same ones an investor asks, and they're in [how to survive VC due diligence meetings](/blog/how-to-survive-vc-due-diligence-meetings).

## How do you test positioning without a big budget?

Put up one landing page per message, with a form on each, and count what people ask for. It's the cheapest experiment you have.

Say you have three ways to describe the product. Make three pages. Send the right people to each one. The question the form answers is simple. What did people come here for?

My [Data-Driven Decision-Making Toolkit](https://gamma.app/docs/Data-Driven-Decision-Making-Toolkit-From-Analytics-to-Visualiza-smz2m6k9t7u666d) has the rules I use to keep tests like this small. Its guideline is to keep every test under two weeks. The tracking side is in [data-driven decision making for startups](/blog/data-driven-decision-making-for-startups).

## What if you only have one customer?

One customer is not your only proof. Your experience, what you know about the problem and the product itself all count.

The founder thought their first customer was probably the only reason anyone would consider them. I don't think that's true. People like social proof, but it comes in more than one form. If you've done the work yourself and you built the thing from what you learned, say so.

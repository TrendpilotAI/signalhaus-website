The founder of a seed-stage AI startup put a question to me on a call. They had limited time, a small team and limited money, and a list of ideas that all looked good. How do you decide where to even start?

I didn't have a clever answer. I had a loop, and it's a simple one. It's two short meetings a week and a spreadsheet.

Here are the questions inside it.

## How do you decide where to start when everything looks important?

Ask where the need is greatest right now, and start there. You can come back to the rest once one thing has settled.

That was the first real question I asked. The founder had already spoken to several advisors and had plenty of advice. So I asked, "Where do you feel the greatest need?"

At this stage everything is a moving target. The pitch deck, the target market, the product and the sales process are all still shifting. You can't fix them all at once, and you don't have to. You need one of them to stop moving so that the others have something to line up behind.

## How do you find the tip of the spear?

Ask the team what they think it is, then pick the one thing a buyer can say yes or no to. The tip of the spear is the narrow thing you lead with so that everything else can follow.

I wouldn't decide this alone. The point is to bring the team together around one focus, so get their input first. Ask everyone the same question. What is the tip of the spear?

You might get more than one answer. That's useful, because now you know what you disagree about, and you can settle it with a small test. Choosing what to lead with is also a positioning question, and I've covered that side in [how to position a startup](/blog/how-to-position-a-startup).

## How do you run a weekly priority loop?

Hold two short meetings a week. One at the start sets the priorities, and one near the end reviews what happened.

The first meeting is on Monday, mid-morning, with everybody in the team. It's 30 minutes and it answers two questions.

- What are the priorities for this week?
- Who is going to focus on what?

The second meeting is on Thursday at a similar time. Each person looks at their own section of a shared spreadsheet and explains it.

- What worked?
- What didn't work?
- Did my numbers go up, or didn't they?

That's the whole loop. The accountability comes from clear lanes. Nobody has to chase anybody, because every number has a name next to it.

Revenue sits at the top as the headline metric. Under it, each person has their own numbers. The spreadsheet takes minutes to build, and everyone can see it all the time.

## How many priorities should a small team have?

One primary goal, with five metrics at most, and one owner for each metric. Those are the guardrails in my [Data-Driven Decision-Making Toolkit](https://gamma.app/docs/Data-Driven-Decision-Making-Toolkit-From-Analytics-to-Visualiza-smz2m6k9t7u666d).

The owner rule is the one I'd hold to hardest. The toolkit's wording is "no shared accountability". If two people share a number, neither of them really owns it.

If you haven't chosen your numbers yet, [data-driven decision making for startups](/blog/data-driven-decision-making-for-startups) walks through picking the first one.

## How do you prioritize with limited time, people and money?

Keep every test small enough to finish quickly, and hand the legwork to AI agents where you can. The toolkit's rule is that a test should take less than two weeks.

"Fix our go-to-market" is too big to start on a Monday. "Put up one landing page for one message and see who fills in the form" is something you can start today.

The agents are what make this workable for a small team. Research, first drafts, outreach and reporting can run during the week and feed results back to the spreadsheet. Then your job changes. You spend more of the week directing the work and less of it grinding through it.

That is what I mean by [Services-as-Software](/services). A small team can get through a lot more work this way, and it stays clear who is doing what.

## When should you drop a priority?

Drop it when the number hasn't moved after two reviews. The toolkit treats that as the signal to stop the experiment or pick a new metric.

It's hard to do, because you chose the priority and you want it to work. A rule helps. You agreed in advance what would count as not working, so stopping is just following the rule.

Then go back to the Monday question. What are the priorities for this week?

## What should you stop worrying about?

Stop worrying that you don't have every answer yet. You're part of a team and you have advisors for exactly that reason.

The founder said at the end of the call that they clearly didn't have all the answers. Earlier I had told them to give themselves a break, and I meant it. Three or four years into my own company I was still asking which numbers mattered, and pricing felt like the Bermuda Triangle.

I think this kind of focus is what investors want to see. A clear priority, a number attached to it and a weekly habit of checking it. The questions they'll ask are in [how to survive VC due diligence meetings](/blog/how-to-survive-vc-due-diligence-meetings).

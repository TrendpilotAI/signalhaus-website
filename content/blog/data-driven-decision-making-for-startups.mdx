I asked the founder of a seed-stage AI startup which number they would most like to see change. They said sales. Then I asked for a specific number, and they told me honestly that they didn't have one.

I told them not to worry. Three or four years into my own company I was the one asking, "What numbers are we looking at? Which metrics matter?"

So if you don't have your numbers yet, you're in good company. This is how I'd start.

## What is data-driven decision making for a startup?

It means you pick a number, write it down every week, and let it change what you do next. That's all it has to be at the start.

You don't need a BI tool or a data hire. A spreadsheet will do. What makes it data-driven is the habit of looking at the same number every week and saying out loud why it moved.

I put the full method into the [Data-Driven Decision-Making Toolkit](https://gamma.app/docs/Data-Driven-Decision-Making-Toolkit-From-Analytics-to-Visualiza-smz2m6k9t7u666d). This post is the short version, with the questions I ask founders along the way.

## How do you choose a North Star metric?

Ask which number you would most like to see change, and take the first honest answer. It can be more than one to begin with, and it can be any kind of number.

That is how I ask it on a call. "What's the most important number to you? What comes to mind?"

The founder's answer was sales, and revenue is a fine headline metric. When you only have a customer or two, though, it can sit still for weeks, and a number that sits still doesn't tell you much.

So the toolkit starts with what it calls a North Star Lite metric. It's the smallest outcome that proves you learned something. These are the ones the toolkit suggests for six different businesses.

- A manufacturer with a small marketing budget counts qualified leads per week.
- A consultancy that matches two groups of people counts matches per month.
- A health founder who is still researching counts new validated pain statements per week.
- A health-tech startup counts demos booked per week.
- An education business counts active accounts per month.
- A startup advisor counts experiments closed per week.

None of those is revenue. Every one of them can be counted this Friday.

## How do you set up tracking in about an hour?

Make one sheet with three columns, which are the date, the number, and one line on why it moved. Then book a short weekly slot to look at it.

The toolkit lays the setup out in six small steps.

1. Pick the one metric.
2. Make the sheet. Date, metric value, one-line insight.
3. Add one no-code automation so new data lands in the sheet without copy and paste.
4. Add a short form with three questions for after each result.
5. Turn the number column into a line chart and share the link where the team already talks.
6. Put a 15-minute review in the calendar for Friday.

Free tools are enough for all of it. The setup is the easy part. Keeping the review is the hard part, and a fixed slot is what stops it drifting to "when we have time".

## What should you ask in the weekly review?

Ask what's working, what isn't, and how the numbers changed. Each person answers for their own numbers.

These are the questions I'd have on the screen.

- What worked this week?
- What didn't?
- How did the number change from last week?
- How does that feed into sales?
- Are opportunities moving through the pipeline?
- Are more leads coming in at the top?

The toolkit adds three more that it calls the Insight Loop. You answer them after each result, while you still remember.

- Why did this happen?
- What will we try next?
- What's in the way?

The answers go in the same row as the number. Then the story stays with the data and nobody has to build slides.

I use the same review to set priorities for the week after. That part is in [how to prioritize as a startup founder](/blog/how-to-prioritize-as-a-startup-founder).

## How many metrics should a startup track?

Five at most, under one primary goal, with one owner for each. The toolkit calls these the KIS guardrails, for Keep It Simple.

It's tempting to add a metric every time someone asks a good question. Each one costs you attention in the review, though. Five is a ceiling. You don't have to reach it.

It also helps to write down what your words mean. The toolkit suggests a shared glossary, so that "retention" or "qualified" means the same thing to everyone in the room.

## How do you know when you have enough data to decide?

Decide what "enough" looks like before you look at the result. The toolkit calls this a decision threshold, and it's there to stop you collecting data forever.

Before the test starts, write one sentence. "If we see this, we do that." Then when the result comes in you already know what it means.

The toolkit also has a question to ask before you trust a metric. "If this number moves but the goal doesn't, why would that happen?" If you can think of an easy way for that to happen, the metric is measuring the wrong thing.

It's a good thing to have ready for an investor too. The questions I'd expect them to ask are in [how to survive VC due diligence meetings](/blog/how-to-survive-vc-due-diligence-meetings).

## How do you run cheap experiments with real data?

Use landing pages. Put up one page per message, add a form, and count what people ask for.

The toolkit's guideline is to keep every test under two weeks and under $500, on a backlog that fits on one page. That's small on purpose, so that tests get finished.

Landing pages are quick to make now. If you have three ways to describe what you sell, you can have three pages up and see which one people respond to. That's a positioning test as much as a data one, and there's more on it in [how to position a startup](/blog/how-to-position-a-startup).

## When should you delete a metric or a chart?

Delete it when it has no goal behind it or when it has stopped moving. The toolkit's rule of thumb is that with no goal there is no metric, and with no metric there is no chart.

There are two checks.

- **Weekly.** Give each number a tick. Green if it's moving the right way, amber if it's flat, red if it's going the other way.
- **Monthly.** Is the goal still live? Does the metric still move? Is the chart still as simple as it can be? If the goal has gone, retire the chart.

And if a number hasn't changed after two reviews, stop the experiment or pick a new number. That's allowed. You're trying to learn something every week, and a number that never moves can't teach you anything.
